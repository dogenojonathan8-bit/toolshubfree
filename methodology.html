Methodology & Data Sources

Last reviewed: August 2026

Why this page exists: every calculator on this site turns published data into an estimate. This page explains where that data comes from, how we turn it into a number, how often we review it, and what our estimates can't account for — so you can judge how much weight to put on a result.

Three kinds of numbers on this site

We try to keep these clearly separate wherever a page displays a number:

  • Official data — a figure published by a government agency or recognized research body (an IRS tax bracket, a Census median income, a Freddie Mac average mortgage rate). We cite the source and the date.
  • SmartToolsHub estimate — a result our calculator produced by applying official data and standard financial formulas to the numbers you entered. It's specific to your inputs and will not exactly match anyone else's real bill, paycheck, or approval.
  • General guideline — a widely used rule of thumb (the 28/36 rule for housing, the 50/30/20 budgeting split, the snowball vs. avalanche debt strategies). These are useful starting points, not requirements any lender or budget must follow.

Where a page cites a source, we're working toward every citation being a clickable link directly to that source rather than a plain text mention — this is an active project, and pages are updated to that standard as we review them (see "Review status" below).

How our calculators do the math

Paycheck calculator (PayCalc Pro)

Applies the current federal income tax brackets and standard deduction to your entered income and filing status, then calculates Social Security (6.2% up to the annual wage base) and Medicare (1.45%, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax above the applicable threshold), then applies your state's income tax brackets where the state has one. The result is gross pay minus these withholdings, before any employer-specific pre-tax or post-tax deductions you may have (401(k), health insurance, etc.), which you can enter separately where the calculator supports it.

Home & car affordability calculators

Use standard debt-to-income (DTI) guidelines — commonly a 28% housing-to-income ratio and a 36% total-debt-to-income ratio for mortgages, and a lower threshold for auto loans — combined with the interest rate, loan term, and costs (property tax, insurance, HOA where applicable) you enter or that we estimate for your area. This produces an estimated affordable purchase price and monthly payment. It is not a loan pre-approval and does not reflect any specific lender's underwriting rules.

Debt payoff calculator

Runs the standard snowball (smallest balance first) and avalanche (highest interest rate first) payoff formulas against the balances, rates, and minimum payments you enter, and projects a payoff timeline for each strategy using standard amortization math.

Cost-of-living and state pages

Combine Census and BLS income/spending data with state and local tax rates to describe what a household in that state or metro area typically pays for housing, taxes, and everyday costs. These are population-level averages, not a quote for any individual household.

What our estimates do not account for

  • Your specific lender's or employer's internal rules, credit decisioning, or approval criteria.
  • Local municipal or county taxes not captured in a state-level tax table.
  • Day-to-day market movement — mortgage rates and home prices can change between our "last reviewed" date and the day you use a calculator.
  • Individual circumstances: your specific deductions, benefits, insurance risk profile, or negotiated pricing.

Review status
